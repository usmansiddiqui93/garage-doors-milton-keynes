Garage Door Weather Seals, Draughts & Condensation

How to keep rain, draughts and pests out of your garage, why condensation happens, and the fixes that genuinely work.

Garage door weather seals close the gaps along the bottom, sides and top of your door to keep out draughts, rain, leaves, dust and mice. Fitting or replacing worn seals is one of the cheapest ways to make a garage drier and more comfortable. Seals alone won't stop condensation, though. Condensation happens when warm, moist air meets a cold surface such as a single-skin steel door, so the fix is usually a combination of good seals, ventilation, reducing moisture and, where it makes sense, an insulated door.

This guide explains the different types of seals for garage doors, how to weather seal a garage door, why garages get condensation and damp, and what actually works to stop it.

Types of garage door weather seals

There are seals for each part of the door, and the right ones depend on your door type.

Bottom seals

The bottom seal does the most work, because it's where rain, wind and leaves get in. Common types include:

  • Rubber or PVC bottom seals fixed to the bottom edge of the door, which compress against the floor when closed.
  • Replaceable seals in an aluminium bottom rail, standard on most sectional and roller doors, which slide out and can be renewed.
  • Brush seals, useful where the floor is uneven.
  • Threshold seals, a raised strip bonded to the floor that the door closes against. Great for driving rain and uneven floors, but they create a small step, so think about trip hazards and bikes.

Side and top seals

  • Perimeter seal kits for up and over doors fit around the frame to close the gap at the sides and top.
  • Brush strips inside roller door guides reduce draughts and rattle.
  • Rubber flap or bulb seals on sectional door frames and lintels seal against the door face.

Modern sectional garage doors come with seals all round as standard, and insulated roller garage doors have a rubber seal in the bottom slat and brushes in the guides. Older up and over doors often have little or no sealing, which is why they tend to be draughty.

Signs your garage door seals need replacing

  • You can see daylight around or under the door.
  • Puddles form inside after rain, or leaves blow in.
  • The rubber is cracked, brittle, flattened or missing in places.
  • The garage is noticeably draughty or dusty.
  • Insects or mice are getting in.

Checking seals is part of our recommended garage door maintenance routine; a quick look each autumn before winter weather is ideal.

Keeping seals for garage doors in good condition

Rubber seals last much longer with a little care. Wipe them over with warm soapy water a couple of times a year to remove grit, which wears the rubber every time the door closes. Avoid oil-based sprays on rubber, as they can make it swell or perish; a silicone-based product is kinder if you want to stop seals sticking in frosty weather. Keep the floor under the door clear of stones and leaves so the bottom seal can close evenly, and check side seals haven't been pushed out of their channels.

How to weather seal a garage door

Fitting simple seals is a reasonable DIY job on many doors. A typical approach:

  1. Identify your door and seal type. Note the make and the profile of any existing seal. Sectional and roller doors often need a seal that matches the bottom rail exactly.
  2. Measure the width of the door and the size of the gaps. Uneven gaps may need a threshold seal or brush rather than a standard rubber strip.
  3. Remove the old seal and clean the surface so new adhesive or fixings hold well.
  4. Fit the new seal following the manufacturer's instructions, keeping it straight and continuous with no gaps at the corners.
  5. Test the door. It should close fully and seal evenly without being forced.
Safety note: Don't let new seals stop the door closing properly or change its balance. On electric doors, a thicker seal or new threshold can affect the motor's limits and safety reversing, so the settings may need adjusting. Never adjust springs, cables or cones yourself; they're under high tension and should be handled by a professional.

Why do garages get condensation?

Garages are often unheated and poorly ventilated, which creates ideal conditions for condensation. Common causes include:

  • Cold door panels: single-skin steel up and over doors get very cold, and warm air condenses on the inside face, then drips onto the floor.
  • Wet cars: a car brought in after rain releases a surprising amount of moisture.
  • Drying washing or a tumble dryer: an unvented dryer in a garage puts a lot of water into the air.
  • Concrete floors: floors without a damp-proof membrane can draw moisture up from the ground.
  • Poor ventilation: sealing a garage tightly without ventilation can make condensation worse.

How to reduce condensation and damp in a garage

Improve ventilation

Air movement carries moisture away. Air bricks, wall vents or a vented side door help. It may seem odd to add vents after fitting seals, but the aim is to stop draughts and rain while allowing controlled air flow.

Reduce moisture at the source

Vent tumble dryers outside, avoid drying washing in the garage, and give a wet car the chance to drip on the drive first where possible.

Use a dehumidifier

If the garage is used as a workshop, gym or storage space, a dehumidifier can keep humidity under control, especially in winter.

Deal with the cold surface

The most effective long-term fix for condensation on the door is to stop the door being so cold. An insulated garage door, such as a foam-filled sectional or roller door, has a warmer inside surface and usually far better seals as well. Our guide are insulated garage doors worth it? looks at whether the upgrade makes sense for you.

DIY insulation panels for up and over doors are available, but they add weight. That can upset the door's balance and put extra strain on springs and any motor. If you add insulation, have the door's counterbalance checked by a professional.

Check the floor and walls

If the floor or lower walls are damp even in dry weather, the problem may be rising damp or a missing damp-proof membrane rather than condensation. That's a job for a builder or damp specialist.

Weather seals on electric garage doors

Electric doors rely on accurate limit settings to close fully onto the seal. If your electric door doesn't seal properly at the bottom, reverses just before closing or leaves a gap, the limits may need resetting rather than the seal replacing. Our guide on what to do when your electric garage door is not working covers common causes.

Frequently asked questions

Straight answers to what people ask us most.

What is the best weather seal for a garage door?

It depends on your door and floor. A rubber or PVC bottom seal suits most doors on a reasonably level floor. A threshold seal fixed to the floor is excellent against driving rain but adds a small step. Brush seals help on uneven floors. Sectional and roller doors usually need a replacement seal that matches the bottom rail profile exactly.

Can I replace garage door seals myself?

On many doors, yes. Bottom seals and simple perimeter kits are a reasonable DIY job if you match the seal to your door. Make sure the door still closes fully and evenly afterwards. On electric doors, new seals can affect the motor's limits and safety reversing, so settings may need adjusting. Never adjust springs or cables yourself.

Why is my garage door dripping with condensation?

Single-skin steel doors get very cold, so warm, moist air in the garage condenses on the inside face and drips down. Wet cars, drying washing and poor ventilation make it worse. Reducing moisture, improving ventilation and using a dehumidifier all help, but the most effective long-term fix is usually an insulated garage door with a warmer inside surface.

Will weather seals stop condensation in my garage?

Not on their own. Weather seals stop draughts, rain, leaves and pests getting in, but condensation is caused by moist air meeting cold surfaces. To reduce condensation you need to cut moisture sources, keep some ventilation, and ideally make the door warmer with insulation. Good seals combined with an insulated door give the best results.

Can you fit seals to an old up and over garage door?

Yes. Bottom seals and perimeter seal kits are available for most up and over doors and can make a noticeable difference to draughts and water ingress. Gaps that are very uneven may need a threshold seal or brush. If the door itself is badly worn or rusted, it may be worth comparing the cost with a new insulated door.
