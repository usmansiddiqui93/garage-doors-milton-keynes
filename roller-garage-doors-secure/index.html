Are Roller Garage Doors Secure?

A plain-speaking look at how secure roller garage doors really are, what makes one door far tougher than another, and what to ask before you buy.

Yes, roller garage doors are secure — provided you choose a well-built, properly fitted one. A good-quality electric roller door with double-skinned, foam-filled laths, deep guide rails and an anti-lift locking system is one of the hardest garage doors for a burglar to get through, and some models are independently tested to recognised security standards such as LPS 1175. A cheap, thin single-skin roller with shallow guides and an exposed manual override, on the other hand, can be weaker than a decent up and over door. The difference is all in the specification and the fitting, and that is what this guide explains.

How secure are roller garage doors compared with other types?

Most garage break-ins are quick and opportunistic. A thief wants to be in and out in a couple of minutes with bikes, tools or garden machinery, so they go for the easiest weak point: a single central lock on an old up and over door, a bent canopy corner that can be levered open, or a flimsy side door. A modern electric roller door removes most of these easy options because the curtain is held down by the motor and locks along its full width rather than at one point.

What makes secure roller garage doors secure?

When two roller doors look almost identical from the driveway, these are the details that separate the tough one from the weak one.

1. The laths (the slats that make up the curtain)

Better roller doors use double-skinned aluminium or steel laths filled with dense insulating foam. The foam does more than keep heat in: it stops the lath being dented, folded or punched through easily. Thicker, deeper lath profiles are generally stronger and are often used on wider doors. Single-skin laths are lighter and cheaper but much easier to deform.

2. Guide rails and end locks

The curtain runs in a guide rail on each side. On a secure door these are deep and strong, and the laths are fitted with end locks or wind locks that stop them being pulled sideways out of the guide. A classic attack on a cheap roller door is to bow the middle of the curtain and pop the laths out of shallow guides, so this detail matters a great deal.

3. Anti-lift protection

With an electric roller door, the tubular motor inside the barrel acts as a brake when the door is closed. Many doors add anti-lift brackets or security straps at the top of the curtain, so that even if someone tries to force the bottom rail upwards, the curtain cannot be wound back on to the barrel.

4. The bottom rail

The bottom slat takes the brunt of any prying attempt. A robust aluminium bottom rail with a good rubber seal sitting snugly on a level floor gives very little to get a bar under. An uneven floor that leaves a gap is an invitation, which is one reason careful surveying and fitting is as important as the door itself.

5. The manual override

Every electric roller door needs a way to open it in a power cut. If the garage has another way in, such as a side door or an internal door into the house, the override should be on the inside. An external override key switch is only used where it is the only way in, and it should be a good-quality, recessed lock. You can read more about overrides and power cuts in how roller garage doors work.

6. Remotes and controls

Modern remotes use rolling-code (or encrypted) signals, so a code captured once cannot simply be replayed. If you are still using an old fixed-code remote, it may be worth upgrading the receiver. Keep remotes out of sight in the car, and never leave a spare in an unlocked vehicle on the drive.

Quick check: stand inside your garage with the door closed. Can you see daylight down the sides? Can you flex the curtain by hand more than a little? Is the bottom rail sitting tight to the floor? If not, the door may be easier to force than it should be.

Security ratings: LPS 1175 and Secured by Design explained

Marketing words like "high security" have no fixed meaning, so independent standards are the best way to compare doors.

  • LPS 1175 is a standard published by the Loss Prevention Certification Board (LPCB). Products are physically attacked in a test lab by trained testers using defined tool sets for set times. Older issues of the standard expressed results as security ratings such as SR1, SR2 and upwards; the latest issue uses a letter-and-number format that describes both the tools used and the time resisted. Either way, a higher rating means the door held out against more determined attack.
  • Secured by Design is the official UK police security initiative. Products that achieve its Police Preferred Specification have been tested to a relevant security standard and the manufacturer has been assessed. For garage doors, LPS 1175 is one of the standards commonly referenced.
  • PAS 24 is mainly used for external doorsets and windows. It is more relevant to a garage side door or a door that will be part of a converted, habitable space than to a standard roller curtain.

Only a minority of domestic roller doors carry these certifications, and they cost more. For most homes, a good-quality, well-specified door from a leading manufacturer is a big step up on its own. Brands such as SWS, whose SeceuroGlide range is well known in the UK roller door market, offer models with tested security; see our page on SWS and SeceuroGlide doors for what is available.

Are electric roller garage doors secure?

Yes — in fact the electric versions are generally more secure than manual roller doors. A manual roller relies on a spring-assisted curtain and a lock, often in the bottom rail, whereas an electric door is held closed by the motor and gearbox at all times. That said, the motor is only part of the picture. The lath quality, guides and override position still decide how hard the door is to beat. Our electric garage doors page covers the motor options and safety features in more detail.

Where roller doors can be vulnerable

To give a balanced answer to "how secure are roller garage doors", it helps to be honest about the weak spots:

  • Budget, single-skin curtains that can be dented or bowed by hand.
  • Shallow or poorly fixed guide rails, especially where fixings go into crumbling brickwork.
  • External override locks that are cheap, exposed or not needed in the first place.
  • Gaps under the bottom rail on sloping or uneven garage floors.
  • Lack of maintenance, such as a motor limit that has drifted so the door no longer closes fully.
  • The rest of the garage — a strong roller door is little help if the side door has a basic lock or the window is single glazed.

How to secure roller garage doors you already have

If your existing door is sound but you want more peace of mind, there are some sensible, low-cost steps:

  1. Ask for a service to check the guides, end locks, limits and bottom seal; small adjustments often close gaps.
  2. Upgrade an old fixed-code receiver to a rolling-code system.
  3. Have anti-lift brackets fitted if your door does not already have them (not every model can take them).
  4. Move an external override inside if there is now another way in.
  5. Add a garage alarm sensor or a floor-mounted locking bar for extra protection when you are away.
  6. Improve the side door, lighting and visibility around the garage.

Secure roller shutter garage doors: what about cost?

A more secure roller door does not have to be a luxury purchase. As a rough guide, a well-specified, insulated electric roller door for a single garage in the UK is typically in the region of £1,400–£2,600 fitted, with tested high-security models and wider doors costing more. These figures are indicative only and are always confirmed after a free survey, because size, lath type, colour, box position and controls all change the price. Frequently asked questions

Straight answers to what people ask us most.

Are roller garage doors more secure than up and over doors?

Generally, yes. A good electric roller door is held closed by its motor and resists lifting across its full width, while a typical up and over door locks at one central point and can be levered at the corners. However, a cheap single-skin roller can be weaker than a well-maintained steel up and over door, so the specification and fitting matter more than the door type alone.

Can a roller garage door be forced open from outside?

A well-built roller door with foam-filled laths, deep guides, end locks and anti-lift protection is very hard to force quickly. The doors that are forced tend to be budget models with thin laths and shallow guides, or doors with a gap under the bottom rail. If you are worried about your existing door, an engineer can check it and suggest improvements such as anti-lift brackets.

What does LPS 1175 mean on a roller garage door?

LPS 1175 is an independent security standard from the Loss Prevention Certification Board. Doors are attacked in a test lab using specified tools for set periods, and they receive a rating based on how long and against which tools they held out. A higher rating means greater resistance. Not every roller door is certified, so ask to see the certification if security is your priority.

Is the manual override on a roller door a security risk?

It can be if it is fitted outside when it does not need to be. Where the garage has another way in, such as a side door or internal door, the override should be fitted inside. If an external override is genuinely needed because there is no other access, it should be a good-quality recessed lock. Our engineers will advise on the best position when we survey your garage.

Does an insulated roller door give better security?

Usually it does. Insulated laths are double skinned and filled with foam, which makes them much stiffer and harder to dent or fold than single-skin laths. The insulation is designed mainly to keep heat in and reduce noise, but the extra rigidity is a useful security benefit too. See our insulated garage doors page for the options.
