Sectional garage doors in Milton Keynes are one of the most requested upgrades we quote for, and it's easy to see why. A sectional door is made of insulated horizontal panels that lift straight up and then run back along the ceiling, so it opens vertically, seals tightly and looks sharp on everything from a 1980s link-detached in Two Mile Ash to a new build in Brooklands or Tattenhoe Park.

We supply, fit, automate and repair sectional doors from leading manufacturers. Here's what you need to know before you choose one.

How a sectional garage door works

Each door is built from four or five horizontal panels hinged together. Rollers on the panel edges run in vertical tracks either side of the opening, which curve over at the top and continue horizontally under the garage ceiling. Torsion springs on a shaft above the opening (or tension springs on the tracks, for lower-headroom set-ups) counterbalance the weight, so the door lifts smoothly by hand or with a ceiling-mounted motor.

Because the door never swings outwards, you can park close to it. Because it travels up inside the frame, it can usually fill the whole width of the opening, which is a real advantage if you have a narrow opening and a wide modern car.

Sectional door options

Panel designs

The panel profile does most of the work visually. Popular styles include:

  • Ribbed (horizontal grooves): the classic look on many UK estates.
  • Medium or large ribbed: wider spacing for a bolder, more industrial feel.
  • Smooth or flush panels: very clean and contemporary, especially in anthracite or black.
  • Cassette or Georgian panels: a more traditional, framed look for older-style homes.

Materials

Most sectional doors are double-skinned steel filled with polyurethane foam, usually around 40–42mm thick. Aluminium sectional doors with glazed sections are available for a striking modern finish, and timber sectional doors (or steel with a real timber face) give the warmth of wood with the operation of a sectional. If you're weighing materials, our guide what are garage doors made of? compares them in plain English.

Colours and surface finishes

White remains common, but black sectional garage doors and anthracite grey (RAL 7016) are now chosen on a large share of the doors we fit. Finishes include smooth, fine-textured and embossed woodgrain, and many manufacturers offer matching side doors.

Windows and glazing

A row of windows in the top panel brings daylight into the garage without giving away what's inside. Options range from small rectangular windows to full glazed sections in aluminium frames.

Insulation

Sectional doors are the go-to choice when insulation matters most. Thicker panels and thermally broken frames are available if your garage is integral to the house or you're turning it into a home gym. See insulated garage doors for how they compare with other types.

Automation and security

Electric sectional garage doors use a belt or chain operator mounted on the ceiling. Typical features include soft start and stop, obstacle detection, a lighting module and anti-lift protection that locks the door when closed. Manual sectional doors use a lockable handle with side bolts or hooks. Read more on our electric garage doors page.

Side sliding sectional doors

Where the ceiling is fully used or the headroom is very low, a side sliding sectional door runs the panels around one side wall instead of overhead. It's a niche option, but it solves some awkward garages and lets you open the door partway for pedestrian access.

Is a sectional door right for you?

Sectional doors make the most sense if you:

  • Have an integral or attached garage and want to cut draughts into the house.
  • Use the garage as a workshop, gym or extra living space.
  • Want a premium, quiet, electric door and don't need the ceiling for storage.
  • Have a double garage: sectional doors are well suited to wide openings. See our page on double garage doors.

They're not ideal where the garage ceiling is low and cluttered with pipework or where you need overhead storage. Hörmann are among the best-known names for sectional doors, and our Hörmann garage doors page outlines their range.

Fitting a sectional door: what to expect

A sectional door is a bigger installation than a like-for-like up and over swap, so our survey is thorough. We measure the opening, headroom, side room and the depth of the garage, and check the ceiling for obstructions such as lights, beams or loft hatches.

On fitting day we remove your old door and frame, install the vertical and horizontal tracks, hang the panels, fit and tension the springs and, if it's electric, install and programme the operator. A single door typically takes most of a day; a double can take a little longer. We finish by testing the safety features and running you through day-to-day use.

If your opening needs reducing or the brickwork needs making good, we'll tell you upfront and include it in the quote.

Sectional door repairs and servicing

Sectional doors are reliable, but springs and cables do wear over time. If a spring snaps the door can become very heavy or refuse to open, and you should stop using it. Broken springs and frayed cables are dangerous to handle, so please leave them to a professional; our spring and cable repair service covers all major makes. We also replace worn rollers, realign tracks and repair operators.

Frequently asked questions

Straight answers to what people ask us most.

How much headroom does a sectional garage door need?

A standard sectional door with torsion springs above the opening usually needs around 200mm or more of headroom between the top of the opening and the ceiling. Low-headroom track systems can work with less, sometimes around 100mm, though the door will be slightly different in construction. We measure your headroom on the free survey and recommend the right track type.

Can I still use my garage ceiling with a sectional door?

Partly. The horizontal tracks and the open door panels sit just below the ceiling, running back roughly the height of the door. You can usually keep storage along the side walls and at the back of the garage, but anything hanging from the ceiling in the door's path will need to move. If overhead storage is essential, a roller door is often the better choice.

Are sectional doors more secure than up and over doors?

In general, yes. A closed sectional door sits tight within its tracks, and many electric operators include anti-lift locking that resists the door being forced upwards. Manual versions use side locking bars. That said, security varies by model, so we're happy to talk through options such as additional locks or security-tested doors when we survey your garage.

Can a sectional door be fitted to a double garage?

Yes, sectional doors are one of the best options for double garages because the panels stay rigid across wide spans and the operator handles the weight easily. If you currently have two single doors with a brick pier between them, we can also advise on removing the pier to create one wide opening, which usually needs structural input.
