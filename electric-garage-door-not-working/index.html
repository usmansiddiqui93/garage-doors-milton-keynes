Electric Garage Door Not Working? A Step-by-Step Guide

Simple, safe checks you can do yourself, how to open the door manually, and when it's time to call an engineer.

  • Manual release explained safely

If you have an electric garage door not working, start with the simple checks: make sure the motor has power (check the plug, fused spur and consumer unit), replace the remote batteries, clean and realign the safety photocells, and look for anything blocking the door or its tracks. Those four checks fix a surprising number of call-outs. If the motor hums but the door won't move, the door stops or reverses part way, or you can see a broken spring or cable, stop using it and call an engineer.

Below is a step-by-step troubleshooting guide covering the most common faults, how to open the door manually, what to do if it opens by itself, and how to reprogram remotes, followed by clear advice on when it's time to call in a professional.

Safety first: Before inspecting the motor or door, switch off and unplug the power. Keep children and pets clear, never stand or walk under a moving door, and don't touch springs, cables or cable drums. These are under high tension and can cause serious injury.

1. Check the power supply

No lights on the motor unit and no response to the remote or wall button usually points to a power problem.

  • Check the motor is plugged in and the socket or fused spur is switched on.
  • Look at your consumer unit for a tripped RCD or circuit breaker. If it trips again straight away, leave it off and call an engineer.
  • Check the fuse in the plug or spur.
  • Try another appliance in the same socket to confirm the supply.

Do garage doors work when the power is out?

Not normally. Most electric garage doors won't operate in a power cut unless they have a battery backup fitted. That's why every electric door has a way to open it by hand: an emergency release on sectional and up and over doors, or a manual override on roller doors. Battery backup units are available for many motors and are worth considering if you rely on the garage for your car.

2. Replace the remote batteries

If the wall button works but the remote doesn't, the remote is the likely culprit. Swap the battery (often a coin cell or small 12V battery; the type is usually printed inside) and try again close to the door. If the range has gradually dropped, a weak battery is the usual reason. If a new battery doesn't help, the remote may need reprogramming or replacing, or the receiver in the motor may be faulty.

3. Check the safety photocells

Most electric doors have a pair of safety photocells, small sensors either side of the opening, that stop or reverse the door if something breaks the beam. Problems with them are one of the most common reasons a door won't close.

  • Wipe the lenses clean with a soft cloth; cobwebs, dust and condensation can block the beam.
  • Check nothing is in the way, such as a bin, a bike or a leaf.
  • Look at the indicator lights. If one is off or flashing, the sensors may have been knocked out of alignment; gently adjust until both show a steady light.
  • Low winter sun shining directly into a sensor can occasionally confuse it.

Never tape over, bypass or disconnect the photocells. They're there to stop the door closing on a person, pet or car.

4. Look for obstructions and damage

Check the tracks, guides and floor for stones, ice, debris or anything stored too close. On roller doors, look for bent slats or a curtain that's come out of its guides. On sectional and up and over doors, check the tracks are straight and the rollers are sitting properly. If something is bent or out of line, don't keep pressing the button, as the motor can make the damage worse.

5. Door stops or reverses part way: limits and force

Electric motors use limit settings to know where fully open and fully closed are, and a force setting that detects resistance. If the door stops short, reverses just before closing, or doesn't seal at the bottom, the limits may have drifted or the door may be stiff enough to trigger the obstruction detection. Some motors have a straightforward re-learn procedure in the manual, but on many roller doors limits are set on the motor inside the barrel and need an engineer. A door that suddenly needs more force often has an underlying mechanical issue, such as worn rollers, a failing spring or a lack of lubrication, so it's worth having it checked rather than just turning the force up.

6. Motor hums, clicks or overheats

  • Hums but doesn't move: could be a failed capacitor, stripped gear, seized door, or a broken spring on a sectional door. Stop and call an engineer.
  • Works then stops after repeated use: many motors have a thermal cut-out to prevent overheating. Leave it for 20–30 minutes and try again.
  • Grinding, burning smell or sparking: switch off at the mains and don't use it.
  • Error code or flashing light: check your motor's manual; the code usually identifies the fault.

How to open an electric garage door manually

If you need to get your car out, you can usually open the door by hand.

  • Sectional and up and over doors: pull the emergency release cord (usually a red handle) to disconnect the door from the motor, then lift the door by hand. Some doors also have an external release with a key.
  • Roller doors: many have a manual override, such as a crank handle or a pull cord, inside the garage. Our guide on how roller garage doors work explains the different types.
Important: Only use the release with the door closed where possible. If a spring or cable is broken, the door may be very heavy or could drop suddenly once disconnected. If the door feels much heavier than usual or doesn't stay where you leave it, lower it carefully and call an engineer.

Garage door opening on its own

A garage door opening on its own is unsettling, but there's usually a simple cause:

  • A remote stuck on, pressed in a pocket or bag, or a spare remote rattling around in a car.
  • A faulty or shorting wall button or wiring.
  • On older fixed-code systems, a neighbour's remote on the same code or radio interference.
  • A fault in the motor's control board or receiver.

Remove the batteries from all remotes to rule them out. If it carries on, unplug the motor and use the door manually until it's been looked at. An upgrade to a modern rolling-code receiver often solves interference issues and improves security.

Reprogramming garage door remotes

Most motors have a learn or program button on the motor head or control box. The usual process is to press the learn button, then press the remote button you want to use within a few seconds; a light on the motor flashes or changes to confirm. The exact steps differ between manufacturers, so check your manual first. You may need to clear old codes if a remote has been lost.

How to program two garage doors to one remote

If both doors use compatible motors, use a multi-button remote and program a different button to each door, following the learn procedure on each motor in turn. If the motors are different makes, you may need a universal remote that's compatible with both, or a receiver upgrade. We can supply and program replacement remotes for most major makes.

Electric garage door not working? When to call an engineer

Basic checks are fine, but some faults need a trained engineer with the right tools. Call one if:

  • a spring or cable is broken, frayed or has come off its drum
  • the door is crooked, off its tracks or the curtain has left its guides
  • the motor hums, grinds, smells of burning, or trips the electrics
  • slats or panels are bent, or the door has been hit by a car
  • the door is very heavy or drops when released from the motor
  • it keeps stopping, reversing or opening on its own after the checks above
  • limits need resetting on a roller door, or the control board shows a fault

Frequently asked questions

Straight answers to what people ask us most.

Why has my electric garage door stopped working?

The most common causes are a loss of power, flat remote batteries, dirty or misaligned safety photocells, or something blocking the door. Check those first. If the motor hums without moving, the door stops or reverses part way, or you can see a broken spring or cable, stop using it and call an engineer, as these need professional repair.

Do electric garage doors work in a power cut?

Most don't, unless a battery backup unit is fitted. Every electric door should have a manual option, though: an emergency release cord on sectional and up and over doors, or a manual override such as a crank handle on many roller doors. Battery backups are available for lots of motors if you need access during power cuts.

Why is my garage door opening by itself?

Usually a remote is being pressed accidentally, a wall button or its wiring is faulty, or on older fixed-code systems another remote or interference is triggering it. Take the batteries out of all remotes to rule them out. If it continues, unplug the motor, use the door manually, and have the receiver and control board checked.

How do I reprogram my garage door remote?

Most motors have a learn button on the motor head or control box. Press it, then press the remote button you want to use within a few seconds, and watch for a confirming light. Steps vary between brands, so check the manual. If you've lost a remote, clear the stored codes first so the missing one no longer works.

Is it safe to use the manual release on my electric garage door?

Generally yes, ideally with the door closed. But if a spring or cable has broken, the door can be very heavy and may drop suddenly once it's disconnected from the motor. If it feels much heavier than normal or won't stay in position, lower it carefully, keep clear and call an engineer rather than forcing it.
